## Blurb

**Where others see doom, he sees opportunity. Hell Difficulty? More like a chance to thrive.**

Nathaniel's bus ride was supposed to be just another boring commute. Wrong. Now, he, 23 fellow passengers, and a corgi named Biscuit, are stuck in a "Hell Difficulty" Tutorial, battling monsters and leveling up to survive.

Easy difficulty, anyone can handle. Normal difficulty, you've got to put up a fight to get by. Hard difficulty is where only the tough ones last. And Hell? That's where you have to be a bit out of your mind!

With his terrifying talent for mana manipulation, Nathaniel decides to invest every stat point into mana. Attribute imbalance be damned. It will either kill him before the monsters and his enemies can, or turn him into one of the most powerful beings within the system.

## Thoughts

*As of writing this review, I've read 11 books of content, up to chapter 922 on Royal Road.*

I finally got through my hatred of present tense enough to read this series. And given I read 11 books' worth, you can probably guess it's pretty good. So yes, yes, Nathaniel in the first book is a bit *too* cold and psychopathic, which makes him a bit more unlikeable, but the author tones this down massively in book two and beyond, to the point where dear Nat actually makes friends. Hell, at this point, you could probably tag the book with "found-family" and not be wrong. Whether this was always the plan or a smart pivot by the author after initial reviews highlighted this character choice as something driving people away, who knows! The pivot is done well, the motivations are solid, and it all ties into the character's backstory very well, so full marks!

So I guess we can talk characters first. No, wait, premise first. Very difficult tutorial. 23 people on a bus. Large cast of characters from chapter one. So in that way, book one focusing much more on Nathaniel makes sense, and the other characters and their importance become things that slowly increase instead of barraging you all at once. Some characters in that core tutorial group are obviously more background characters (like the twins), and some are more foreground (Tess, Lily, future-Absolute-overlord Biscuit). All of them, over the course of the many books, develop distinct personalities, with their own motivations and goals.

On that note, dialogue is good. There are sections with top-quality banter and strong character voices. There are some sections where Nathaniel's brusque conversational style is present in many other characters, but this happens less and less as the series goes on.

The system is well implemented. Stupid stats like HP and mana are thankfully left out. Stats (strength, dexterity, etc.) are there and suffer from the normal LitRPG problem of "when big number get too big, how make sense?" Skills are where the system shines, especially with skill combinations, as they allow the characters to keep trying new things in combat instead of things getting stale too quickly.

Though... caveat... I have ended up skimming quite a few "fluff fights" which go on for many pages but don't really move the plot. Fights just for an achievement and without emotional stakes, for example.

Aaand that brings me to what I think is one of the slightly lacking areas of the story. We are in book eleven. Big books. More words than Cradle. And the MC and his group are **STILL IN THE TUTORIAL**. I want more PLOT PROGRESSION. And while the worldbuilding is vast, some of it does end up feeling a bit like the author wanted to do X and thus added it in. Minor worldbuilding spoilers (not plot or character spoilers) ahead. For example:

* The tutorial has 13 floors and goes for five years.
* Each floor can have one or more objectives to complete to move on.
* But also, every year there is a tournament.
* Each tournament has multiple rounds, each a different event or scenario.
* The MC finds his way to a higher difficulty: Beyond.
* There are three scenarios to get into it.
* Once you're there, you enter the First Dungeon.
* The First Dungeon has numerous levels to progress through.
* Each level has its own scenarios to pass.
* The MC can be given quests, which are effectively their own impromptu scenarios.

What this means is that there is an absolute glut of "Oh, we're now preparing for this random thing for this random reason" and sure, it's fun, it's imaginative, but I just can no longer invest in some of them because there have been dozens and dozens of random-ass scenarios in these books already. When it ties into the worldbuilding, power plays, and Earth itself, great. But damn, boss, I am tired. I have event fatigue. Please get to Earth soon.

Now, sure, tons of LitRPG books have these pain points. Every single dungeon-delver story. Path of Ascension can get there in rift-heavy sections. Maybe I am just a bit tired of it after 11 straight books of it and need a palate cleanser. Anyway, moral of the story: it's a very fun, action-heavy, weak-to-strong-into-power-fantasy story that I recommend you check out. Cerim even made a Biscuit plushie, and all stories which are successful enough to have a plushie have got to be good, right?
